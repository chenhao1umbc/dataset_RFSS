\begin{abstract}
% ============================================================
Cellular standards from 2G to 5G now share spectrum, and receivers increasingly
see several of them at once. Source separation could help, but there has been
no dataset for training and testing data-driven methods on this task. We present
RFSS (RF Signal Source Separation), 100,000 multi-source samples generated from
3GPP physical-layer specifications. Each sample mixes 2 to 4 sources from GSM
(3GPP TS~45.004), UMTS (TS~25.211), LTE (TS~36.211) and 5G New Radio (NR,
TS~38.211). Every source passes through its own 3GPP Tapped Delay Line (TDL)
multipath fading channel~\cite{3gpp38901} and a chain of hardware impairments:
carrier frequency offset, I/Q imbalance, phase noise, DC offset and power
amplifier nonlinearity (Rapp model). The sources are mixed either co-channel or
adjacent-channel. A companion file holds 4,000 single-source reference samples.
The multi-source file is 103~GB in HDF5, split 70/15/15 by index.

We benchmark FastICA, Frobenius-norm NMF and three deep architectures (Conv-TasNet~\cite{luo2019conv},
DPRNN~\cite{luo2020dual} and an STFT-domain BLSTM mask estimator)
with permutation-invariant scale-invariant signal-to-interference-plus-noise
ratio (PI-SI-SINR)~\cite{leroux2019sdr}, and report the improvement over the input
mixture. Each deep model uses the learning rate chosen on the validation split.
On the two-source test split the best deep model improves PI-SI-SINR by
6.1~dB over the input, where an ideal-ratio-mask oracle reaches
8.0~dB and ICA and NMF reach $-13.6$ and $-1.2$~dB.
The dataset and evaluation code will be released publicly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

Cellular generations now coexist in shared or adjacent frequency
bands~\cite{cabric2004implementation,mitola1999cognitive}. Legacy GSM networks
still carry machine-type traffic, LTE dominates the mid bands, and 5G NR
deployments overlap both below 6~GHz. The result is persistent co-channel and
adjacent-channel interference, which receivers built for a single standard
handle poorly~\cite{andrews2014what}.

RF source separation recovers the individual signals from one observed mixture.
Spectrum sensing~\cite{haykin2005cognitive,goldsmith2009breaking,axell2012sensing}
only identifies which signals are present and of what type; separation
reconstructs each waveform so that a receiver can demodulate it per standard.
Reliable separation would allow multi-standard receivers, better spectrum
monitoring and interference coordination in cognitive radio.

Deep learning is now widely used for physical-layer tasks such as modulation
classification and end-to-end link design~\cite{oshea2017introduction,west2017deep,oshea2018over}.
Applying it to separation needs training data that covers multi-standard
mixtures under realistic propagation and hardware effects, and that data was not
available for cellular standards.

The existing public RF datasets were built for modulation recognition.
RadioML~\cite{oshea2016radio,oshea2016convolutional} provides single-signal
samples over synthetic modulation types. It has no multi-standard mixtures and no
per-source ground truth, so a separator trained on it would have nothing to
optimize against. Other work classifies the signals of individual standards on a
single channel~\cite{rajendran2018deep}, and GNU Radio~\cite{blossom2004gnu}
generates waveforms, but neither supplies separation ground truth. In audio,
WSJ0-2mix drove the development of Conv-TasNet, DPRNN and related architectures. No
public RF separation dataset covers several cellular standards with per-source
references. RFSS provides one, with the following features.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item It has 100,000 multi-source samples covering GSM, UMTS, LTE and 5G NR,
    enough to train deep models from scratch, with variation in channel type,
    source count and SNR. Each sample stores the per-source waveforms from which
    the exact mixture terms can be rebuilt, which supplies the supervised signal
    for permutation-invariant training.
  \item Each standard is generated from its physical-layer specification instead
    of as filtered noise or a generic modulation: GMSK shaping (TS~45.004),
    Gold-code scrambled Code Division Multiple Access (CDMA) (TS~25.213),
    Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM) with the exact cyclic-prefix
    durations of TS~36.211 Table~6.12-1, and the flexible numerology of
    TS~38.211.
  \item Five hardware impairments use parameter ranges informed by 3GPP
    base-station conformance specifications (TS~38.104, TS~36.104, TS~25.104)
    and are applied independently to each source, as they would be with several
    separate transmitters.
  \item Co-channel and adjacent-channel mixtures are both included.
    Co-channel mixtures test whether overlapping waveforms can be separated at
    all. Adjacent-channel mixtures shift the sources in frequency by multiples of
    2~MHz, which partly separates them spectrally (many sources are wider than the
    shift), so results can show how much of a method's score comes from spectral
    cues and how much from waveform-level separation.
  \item Classical and deep methods are evaluated under one protocol:
    FastICA, NMF, Conv-TasNet, DPRNN and an STFT-domain BLSTM (a CNN-LSTM and a Conv-TasNet
    variant are scored on validation data only). Each deep
    model is trained at the learning rate chosen on the validation split, and the
    results include an ideal-ratio-mask oracle as an upper reference.
  \item The dataset, generation code, trained checkpoints and evaluation scripts
    are released for non-commercial use.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related}
reviews related datasets and prior work. Section~\ref{sec:construction}
describes the dataset construction pipeline. Section~\ref{sec:characterization}
characterizes the resulting dataset. Section~\ref{sec:benchmark} presents
benchmark experiments and results. Section~\ref{sec:access} describes data
access and format. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses limitations and
future work. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\subsection{RF Datasets for Signal Intelligence}

The RadioML datasets~\cite{oshea2016radio,oshea2016convolutional,
oshea2018over} are single-signal recordings with varying modulation type and
SNR, and they are the standard benchmark for automatic modulation
classification (AMC). They are not derived from 3GPP specifications, so GSM,
UMTS, LTE and NR do not appear as categories. They also contain no mixtures of
two or more simultaneous transmissions, and so no ground truth for separation.

DARPA's Spectrum Collaboration Challenge (SC2)~\cite{mennes2020sc2} was about
autonomous radios sharing spectrum cooperatively, which is a resource-sharing
problem and not baseband signal recovery. Work on distributed spectrum
sensing~\cite{rajendran2018deep} classifies signals instead of separating them
and does not cover the full 2G--5G suite. GNU Radio~\cite{blossom2004gnu}
generates waveforms flexibly but offers no multi-standard mixing, ground-truth
labeling or 3GPP compliance checks.

\subsection{Audio Source Separation Datasets}

Audio source separation research relies on shared benchmark datasets. WSJ0-2mix~\cite{hershey2016deep} introduced the two-speaker
mixture setup and motivated permutation-invariant training
(PIT)~\cite{kolbaek2017multitalker}. WHAM!~\cite{wichern2019wham} added realistic
noise, and MUSDB18~\cite{rafii2017musdb18} provides music with four stems. As in
RFSS, every mixture carries per-source references, so models can be trained with
a PIT loss and scored with SI-SNR~\cite{leroux2019sdr}. We use the same protocol
for RF signals. The SI-SINR used here is the SI-SNR of
\cite{leroux2019sdr}, extended to complex signals with a real projection coefficient. We call it SINR because interference, more than additive
noise, is what degrades a mixture of coexisting standards.

Conv-TasNet~\cite{luo2019conv} and DPRNN~\cite{luo2020dual} were developed on
these audio benchmarks. Transformer-based models such as
SepFormer~\cite{subakan2021sepformer} reached 22.3~dB SI-SNRi on WSJ0-2mix with dynamic mixing. We keep
Conv-TasNet and DPRNN as established baselines to test whether audio
architectures transfer to RF without domain-specific changes, and add an
STFT-domain mask estimator.

\subsection{RF Source Separation Prior Work}

Classical RF source separation relies on Independent Component Analysis (ICA) or
Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF). ICA~\cite{hyvarinen2000ica} exploits the
statistical independence of the sources and is a standard blind separation
technique. NMF~\cite{fevotte2011nmf} factors the magnitude Short-Time Fourier
Transform (STFT) into spectral bases and is a common tool in audio separation.

Neither approach learns from a large labeled corpus, which limits how well
it adapts to the modulations and channel conditions of 2G--5G coexistence. Both
work on short fixed windows (Hankel frames for ICA, STFT frames for NMF) and lack
the learned long-range temporal structure of deep models.

The closest dataset-and-benchmark effort is the RF
Challenge~\cite{lancho2024rfchallenge}, a competition at ICASSP~2024 on
interference rejection. Its task differs from ours. Each mixture has one desired
signal with a known generative process, either QPSK or OFDM-QPSK, and one unknown
interferer drawn from four signal types: an electromagnetic-interference source, two
commercial communication devices and a 5G-compliant waveform. The goal is to
recover the desired signal, scored by bit error rate and mean squared error, so
there is no permutation ambiguity and the receiver knows the desired signal type.
RFSS poses the blind problem: every source is an unknown standard-compliant
waveform, the source count varies from 2 to 4, and the separator has to resolve
the permutation. RFSS also generates every source from the 3GPP specifications and
applies TDL fading and five hardware impairments to each of them.

To our knowledge, no earlier work addresses blind separation of 2--4 simultaneous
3GPP-standard sources at this scale, and no labeled training corpus existed. Without
one, researchers cannot train end-to-end separators, compare methods on a common
test split, or tell whether a gap between methods is real or an artifact of a small
evaluation set.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:construction}
% ============================================================

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} illustrates the complete dataset construction
pipeline. Each sample is produced by: (1) selecting 2--4 source standards
at random; (2) generating per-source baseband waveforms from 3GPP parameters;
(3) passing each waveform through an independent TDL channel and hardware
impairment chain; (4) mixing the impaired signals either co-channel or
adjacent-channel; and (5) storing each channel- and impairment-distorted source
waveform, at its native rate and before resampling, frequency shift and power
scaling, alongside the mixture. The exact per-source mixture terms used as
separation targets are rebuilt from these stored waveforms and the metadata
(Section~\ref{sec:access}).

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_pipeline.pdf}
  \caption{RFSS dataset construction pipeline. Each sample passes through
  independent per-source signal generation, 3GPP TDL channel modeling,
  hardware impairment injection, and two-mode mixing before ground-truth
  targets and the mixture observation are written to HDF5.}
  \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Signal Generation}

Each waveform is generated at its native sample rate according to the
3GPP physical-layer specification for that standard: GSM at 2.166~MHz,
UMTS at 7.68~MHz, LTE at 1.92--30.72~MHz depending on bandwidth configuration,
and 5G~NR at 15.36--122.88~MHz depending on numerology and bandwidth.
After per-source generation, all sources in a sample are resampled to a
common mixture rate equal to the maximum source rate in that sample,
ensuring no aliasing and preserving the full bandwidth of the widest source.

\textbf{GSM (2G).} GMSK modulation per 3GPP TS~45.004~\cite{3gpp45004}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
s_{\text{GSM}}(t) &= \exp\!\bigl(j\phi(t)\bigr), \\
\phi(t) &= \pi h \sum_k a_k \int_{-\infty}^{t} g(\tau - kT_s)\,\mathrm{d}\tau
\end{split}
\label{eq:gsm}
\end{equation}
where $h\!=\!0.5$ is the modulation index, $a_k \in \{-1,+1\}$ are
differentially encoded bits, $g(t)$ is the Gaussian pulse shaping filter with
bandwidth-time product $BT\!=\!0.3$, and $T_s\!=\!3.69\,\mu$s is the symbol
duration (270.833~ksps). The exponential form shows that GMSK has a constant
envelope: the signal enters only through the cumulative phase $\phi(t)$. The
channel bandwidth is the 200~kHz specified in TS~45.004.

\textbf{UMTS (3G).} W-CDMA per 3GPP TS~25.211~\cite{3gpp25211}:
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{UMTS}}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm users}}
  \sqrt{P_i} \sum_k d_i[k]\, c_i[k]\, s_i[k]\; p(t - kT_c)
\label{eq:umts}
\end{equation}
where $P_i$ is per-user power, $d_i[k]$ are data symbols, $c_i[k]$ are Orthogonal Variable Spreading Factor (OVSF)
channelization codes, $s_i[k]$ is the Gold-code scrambling sequence per
TS~25.213~\cite{3gpp25213}, and $p(t)$ is the root-raised-cosine chip pulse with rolloff
$\alpha\!=\!0.22$ and chip duration $T_c\!=\!260.4$~ns (3.84~Mcps).
Channel bandwidth is 5~MHz.

\textbf{LTE (4G).} OFDM per 3GPP TS~36.211~\cite{3gpp36211}:
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{LTE}}(t) = \sum_{l=0}^{N_{\rm symb}-1}
  \sum_{k=0}^{N_{\rm SC}-1} X_l[k]\,
  e^{j2\pi k\Delta f(t - lT_s - T_{\rm CP})}
\label{eq:lte}
\end{equation}
where $X_l[k]$ are QAM symbols on subcarrier $k$ of OFDM symbol $l$,
$\Delta f\!=\!15$~kHz is subcarrier spacing, and $T_{\rm CP}$ is the cyclic
prefix duration as specified in TS~36.211 Table~6.12-1. The dataset draws the channel
bandwidth from 1.4, 3, 5, 10, 15 and 20~MHz (FFT size 1024 at 10~MHz).

\textbf{5G NR (5G).} Flexible-numerology OFDM per 3GPP
TS~38.211~\cite{3gpp38211}:
\begin{equation}
s_{\rm NR}(t) = \sum_{l=0}^{N_{\rm symb}-1}
  \sum_{k=0}^{N_{\rm SC}-1} X_l[k]\,
  e^{j2\pi k\Delta f_{\rm SCS}(t - lT_s^\mu - T_{\rm CP}^\mu)}
\label{eq:nr}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta f_{\rm SCS}\!=\!15\!\times\!2^\mu$~kHz is the subcarrier
spacing for numerology $\mu$. The dataset draws $\mu \in \{1,3\}$ (30 or
120~kHz SCS) with bandwidths of 10 to 100~MHz (sample rates up to 122.88~MHz), with
cyclic-prefix durations per TS~38.211 Table~5.3.1-2.

\subsection{Channel Modeling}

Each source signal passes through an independent single-input single-output
(SISO) channel drawn from the 3GPP TDL family defined in
TR~38.901~\cite{3gpp38901}: TDL-A and TDL-B are non-line-of-sight delay
profiles; TDL-C is the primary NLOS wideband profile; TDL-D and TDL-E are
Rician (line-of-sight) profiles with K-factors of 13.3~dB and 22~dB
respectively. Channel type is selected from a weighted distribution
per source per sample, with weights
$[0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.20, 0.20]$ for TDL-A through TDL-E.

Time-varying Doppler is generated using Jakes' sum-of-sinusoids
model~\cite{jakes1994microwave}, with maximum Doppler frequency drawn from
weighted range categories up to 700~Hz, spanning pedestrian (0--10~Hz),
urban (30--120~Hz), highway (150--300~Hz), and high-speed rail (400--700~Hz)
scenarios. AWGN with SNR drawn from weighted range categories from $-10$ to
40~dB is added after channel filtering.

The mixture model combining $N_{\rm src}$ impaired sources is:
\begin{equation}
y(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm src}} \sqrt{P_i}
  \sum_{l=0}^{L_i-1} h_{i,l}(t)\,
  s_i(t - \tau_i - \tau_{i,l})\,
  e^{j2\pi f_i t} + n(t)
\label{eq:mixture}
\end{equation}
where $P_i$ is the power scaling factor for source $i$, $h_{i,l}(t)$ is
the $l$-th tap of its TDL channel impulse response, $\tau_i$ is a
differential timing offset, $f_i$ is the carrier frequency shift
(zero for co-channel, a multiple of 2~MHz for adjacent-channel), and $n(t)$
is additive white Gaussian noise.

\subsection{Hardware Impairments}

Hardware impairments are informed by 3GPP conformance requirements and are applied
independently to each source before
mixing~\cite{3gpp38104,3gpp36104,3gpp25104}.
For each source, the impairment application mode is drawn from a weighted
distribution: approximately 20\% of sources receive no impairments (clean),
30\% receive a single impairment type, and 50\% receive multiple impairments
simultaneously:

\textit{Carrier Frequency Offset (CFO).} A multiplicative phase rotation
$e^{j2\pi f_{\rm CFO} t}$ with $f_{\rm CFO}$ drawn from $\pm0.05$--$5$~ppm
of the carrier, informed by TS~38.104.

\textit{I/Q Imbalance.} Amplitude mismatch 0.1--3~dB and phase error
$1^\circ$--$10^\circ$, informed by the conformance requirements of
TS~36.104.

\textit{Phase Noise.} A Wiener-process phase perturbation with spectral
density $-110$ to $-90$~dBc/Hz at 10~kHz offset, informed by TS~25.104.

\textit{DC Offset.} A constant complex additive offset at $-40$ to
$-30$~dBc relative to signal power, modeling local-oscillator leakage.

\textit{PA Nonlinearity.} The Rapp model~\cite{rapp1991effects} with
input back-off drawn from 3 to 9~dB below the amplifier saturation
point, producing AM/AM and AM/PM distortion.

\subsection{Mixing Scenarios}

Two mixing modes are generated.

\textbf{Co-channel mixing.} All sources are placed at baseband (no frequency
shift: $f_i\!=\!0$ for all $i$). Sources share the entire spectral region,
so separation must rely on differences in modulation type, temporal structure
and statistics. This is the hardest scenario, so the results report it separately.

\textbf{Adjacent-channel mixing.} Source $k$ of a sample with $N_{\rm src}$ sources is
frequency-shifted by $(k-\lfloor N_{\rm src}/2\rfloor)\times 2$~MHz before summing, the same
spacing for every standard (offsets $\{-2,0\}$ MHz for two sources, $\{-2,0,2\}$ for three,
$\{-4,-2,0,2\}$ for four). The spacing is smaller than the bandwidth of most UMTS, LTE and NR
sources, so the sources are displaced but usually still overlap in frequency
(by the nominal channel bandwidths in the metadata, at least one pair of source bands overlaps in 97\% of the two-source, 99.5\% of the three-source and 99.9\% of the four-source adjacent-channel samples; \texttt{check/adjacent\_overlap.py}); the mode is
named for the offset and not for non-overlapping channels.

The number of simultaneous sources $N_{\rm src}$ is drawn from $\{2, 3, 4\}$
with target sampling weights $[0.50, 0.35, 0.15]$, chosen to oversample
easier configurations for training stability. The realized empirical
weights across the 100,000-sample corpus are 0.49, 0.34, and 0.15.
Each sample covers about 1~ms at the native source rate. After resampling to the
common mixture rate (the maximum of all source rates in that sample), the length
stored in \texttt{signal\_lengths} ranges from 1,890 to 122,880 samples (5G~NR at
122.88~MHz).

\section{Dataset Characterization}
\label{sec:characterization}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Composition}

The RFSS dataset comprises 100,000 multi-source samples partitioned into
training (samples 0--69,999; 70\%), validation (70,000--84,999; 15\%), and
test (85,000--99,999; 15\%) splits. An additional 4,000 single-source samples
are provided in a separate file (\texttt{rfss\_single.h5}) for signal
characterization and per-standard classification experiments.

Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_stats} shows the distribution of source counts,
mixing modes, and standard combinations across the corpus. Source count
follows an approximately geometric distribution (heavier weight on smaller
counts), and mixing modes are weighted toward adjacent-channel (40\% co-channel,
60\% adjacent-channel). All
$\binom{4}{k}$ standard combinations for $k \in \{2,3,4\}$ are represented.

\subsection{Signal Properties}

Figure~\ref{fig:spectrograms} presents representative STFT spectrograms for
each of the four standards, illustrating the distinct time-frequency
signatures that a separator must disentangle. GSM exhibits a narrow-band
(200~kHz), constant-envelope character with quasi-stationary spectral
occupancy. UMTS spreads energy across 5~MHz with visible pseudo-noise
spreading gain. LTE and 5G NR display OFDM rectangular spectra with
subcarrier spacings of 15~kHz and 30~kHz respectively, and transient
variation across slots due to PDSCH scheduling.

Separability is not uniform across standard pairs. The greatest inter-standard
spectral contrast exists between GSM (200~kHz narrowband GMSK) and LTE or
5G~NR (wideband OFDM): these combinations provide strong frequency-domain
cues that even classical spectral methods can partially exploit. LTE with
5G~NR should be the hardest pairing: both use OFDM and differ only in subcarrier
spacing and bandwidth, which takes fine time-frequency resolution to tell apart.
We expect co-channel LTE--NR pairs to be harder than pairs of GMSK with an OFDM
standard; we have not measured this per pair.

the near-constant envelope of GSM contrasted with the Rayleigh-like tails
of LTE and 5G NR, as expected from the central-limit behavior of OFDM
symbols.

The 5--6~dB PAPR gap between OFDM and GMSK gives RF mixtures amplitude cues that
audio mixtures lack. The envelope of a GMSK source is constant before the channel, whereas an OFDM
envelope fluctuates strongly, so for short intervals the mixture amplitude can be
dominated by one source. The same envelope also makes the amplitude ratio between sources
vary a great deal within a single training crop. The configured bandwidths span
200~kHz (GSM) to 100~MHz (5G~NR), a factor of 500. The mixture rate of each sample is
the maximum of its source rates and can reach 122.88~MHz.

\subsection{HDF5 Format and Metadata}

The dataset is stored in two HDF5 files using gzip level-6 compression,
totaling 103~GB. The multi-source file (\texttt{rfss\_dataset.h5}) contains
four datasets:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{mixed\_signals}, shape $(100000,\, 122880)$, complex64:
    the observed mixture waveform for each sample.
  \item \texttt{source\_signals}, shape $(100000,\, 4,\, 122880)$,
    complex64: per-source waveforms after the channel and hardware
    impairments, at their native sample rate, zero-padded in time to 122,880
    samples and to a maximum of four sources. They are not the final mixture
    terms: resampling to the mixture rate, the adjacent-channel frequency shift
    and the power scaling are applied by the released
    \texttt{build\_aligned\_references} function.
  \item \texttt{signal\_lengths}, shape $(100000,)$, int32: the length of
    the resampled mixture signal for each sample, supporting variable-length
    signals within the fixed array.
  \item \texttt{metadata}, shape $(100000,)$, variable-length JSON
    string: per-sample metadata including source count, standard
    identifiers, mixing mode, SNR values, channel type, and hardware
    impairment parameters.
\end{itemize}

The flat array structure (no nested groups) keeps HDF5 indexing simple for
random-access data loading.
All complex samples are stored as pairs of float32 (real, imaginary).

\section{Benchmark Experiments}
\label{sec:benchmark}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Evaluation Metric}
\label{sec:metrics}

We score every method with Permutation-Invariant Scale-Invariant
Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio (PI-SI-SINR), following Le Roux et
al.~\cite{leroux2019sdr}. For a reference source $\mathbf{s}$ and an
estimated signal $\hat{\mathbf{s}}$, SI-SINR is:
\begin{equation}
\text{SI-SINR}(\hat{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{s})
  = 10\log_{10}\frac{\|\alpha\mathbf{s}\|^2}
    {\|\hat{\mathbf{s}} - \alpha\mathbf{s}\|^2},\quad
  \alpha = \frac{\mathrm{Re}\{\hat{\mathbf{s}}^{H}\mathbf{s}\}}{\|\mathbf{s}\|^2}
\label{eq:sisinr}
\end{equation}
where mean subtraction is applied to both signals before the projection to remove
DC bias. The projection coefficient $\alpha$ is a real scalar, so a constant phase
rotation of the estimate lowers the score: the metric is phase sensitive.
Permutation invariance is achieved by taking the permutation of the model outputs
that maximizes the mean SI-SINR over the sources.

The references are the exact per-source terms of the mixture: each stored source
waveform is resampled to the mixture rate, frequency shifted (adjacent-channel
mixtures) and power scaled by the released \texttt{build\_aligned\_references}
function. On 1,000 checked samples the residual between the mixture and the sum of
these references has the power implied by the stored SNR to within 0.2~dB.

We report two quantities. The absolute PI-SI-SINR is the score of the separated
estimates. The gain is the absolute score minus the input score, where the input
score is the mean over the sources of SI-SINR(mixture, source). The mixture contains
every source, so the input score is finite ($-4.4$, $-9.3$ and $-12.0$~dB on average over the
2-, 3- and 4-source test samples). The gain is the main figure because the input level
depends on source count, mixing mode and SNR. For context, well-performing speech
separation systems reach about 15~dB SI-SNR improvement on
WSJ0-2mix~\cite{luo2019conv}.

\subsection{Reference Methods}

\textbf{Oracles.} The noise-limited oracle returns each reference plus the noise
residual (the mixture minus the sum of the references); it is the ceiling for perfect separation that leaves all additive noise in
every output. The ideal ratio mask (IRM) oracle applies masks built from the
reference magnitudes on a 2048-point STFT (hop 512) to the mixture STFT and keeps
the mixture phase. It bounds what time-frequency masking can reach.

\textbf{FastICA.} We construct a Hankel (time-delay) embedding matrix
from the scalar observed mixture, increasing the effective number of
observations through lagged copies. The window length is 256 samples
($\approx\!8.3$~$\mu$s at 30.72~MHz) with hop 128 samples, giving a
feature matrix of dimension $n_{\rm frames} \times 512$ (interleaved real
and imaginary channels), with convergence tolerance $10^{-4}$ and up to
500 iterations. The recovered components are matched to the reference sources by
the permutation that maximizes SI-SINR. ICA assumes statistically independent,
non-Gaussian sources, which holds only roughly for cellular signals and becomes
weaker when sources have similar spectra.

\textbf{Frobenius-norm NMF.} The magnitude STFT of the mixture is
factored into $N_{\rm src}$ spectral bases and activations
using NMF with Frobenius norm ($\beta\!=\!2$, 500 iterations, one component per
source). Each source is reconstructed by Wiener-ratio masking in the STFT domain.
NMF can exploit differences in spectral shape, such as GSM's narrow band against
LTE's wide rectangular spectrum. The mask reuses the mixture phase, so NMF quality is
bounded by the spectral diversity of the sources.

\subsection{Deep Learning Methods}

Four architectures, five models counting the Conv-TasNet variant, are trained from scratch on the RFSS training split.

\textbf{Conv-TasNet}~\cite{luo2019conv} uses a learned 1-D convolutional
encoder, a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) of dilated depthwise
convolutions that computes the source masks, and a learned decoder. We use
$N\!=\!256$ encoder filters, kernel length $L\!=\!16$, bottleneck dimension
$B\!=\!128$, hidden channels $H\!=\!256$, kernel size $P\!=\!3$, $X\!=\!8$
blocks per repeat and $R\!=\!3$ repeats ($24$ TCN blocks). A sigmoid gives the
masks. A second variant, Conv-TasNet-L256 (a validation-only pilot, seed 0), uses $L\!=\!256$ with stride 64 and is
otherwise identical.

\textbf{DPRNN}~\cite{luo2020dual} splits the encoder output into
overlapping chunks and alternates intra-chunk and inter-chunk
Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) layers. Configuration: $N\!=\!64$
encoder filters, $L\!=\!16$, feature dimension $B\!=\!64$, hidden
dimension $H\!=\!64$, chunk size $P\!=\!50$ encoder frames, 6 dual-path
layers, sigmoid masks.

\textbf{CNN-LSTM} (a validation-only pilot, seed 0) is an encoder-decoder baseline. Three convolutional layers
with channels [64, 128, 256] (kernel size 7, stride 2, batch normalization and ReLU)
extract local features, and a 2-layer BiLSTM with hidden size 256 models the sequence.
A decoder of three transposed convolutions (kernel 8, stride 2) mirrors the encoder,
and a 1$\times$1 convolution estimates each source waveform directly (regression, no
masking). Our first version used a 1$\times$1 convolution followed by linear
interpolation. Every output of that decoder is piecewise linear between samples eight
apart, which caps the attainable SI-SINR at 1.98~dB on average over 300 training
crops, so we replaced it. We report the first version only as a diagnostic.

\textbf{STFT-BLSTM} computes a 2048-point STFT (hop 512) of the complex mixture and
passes the log magnitude through a linear layer and a 2-layer BiLSTM with hidden size
256. It predicts a complex ratio mask per source (tanh on the real and imaginary
parts), applies it to the mixture STFT and inverts the result.

The two-source models have 2.52~M (Conv-TasNet), 2.77~M (Conv-TasNet-L256), 1.11~M (DPRNN),
4.30~M (CNN-LSTM) and 7.36~M (STFT-BLSTM) parameters; the output layers of the 3- and 4-source models are larger (Table~\ref{tab:cost}).

All models are trained with permutation-invariant training
(PIT)~\cite{kolbaek2017multitalker}, minimizing the negative SI-SINR of the best
permutation. Optimization uses Adam, gradient clipping at norm 1.0, batch size 8 and
cosine annealing to $\eta_{\min}\!=\!10^{-5}$ over $E\!=\!10$ epochs. Input crops of
7,680 samples are redrawn at random every epoch. The learning rate is chosen per
model on the validation split (Table~\ref{tab:lr}) and is not shared across models. In an earlier run at $10^{-3}$ the Conv-TasNet did not leave a plateau within 30 epochs. In
a validation sweep with otherwise identical settings it stayed at a gain of about
2.5~dB over the first two epochs at $10^{-3}$ and left that plateau in the first epoch
at $3\times10^{-4}$. For the STFT-BLSTM at $10^{-3}$, whether the model left the plateau
depended on the seed. We therefore choose the learning rate with the higher worst-seed validation
gain at the common epoch budget. The checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is
evaluated once on the test split. Each model trains for $E$ passes over the training subset
with its source count, so the 3-source and 4-source models see 0.70 and 0.30 times the
optimizer steps of the 2-source model (about 4,364, 3,069 and 1,318 steps per epoch for the
34,912, 24,547 and 10,541 training samples). All experiments use single-channel (SISO)
signals.

\textbf{Flagged cells.} The 3-source and 4-source cells were checked against the 2-source curve of the same model: at the same
number of optimizer steps, a validation gain more than $0.5$~dB below the (interpolated) 2-source gain, or a rise of less than $0.3$~dB since epoch 2,
flagged the cell and triggered a validation-only screen of neighbouring learning rates. The 3- and 4-source tasks are harder, so the reference is a diagnostic and not a target, and
no cell was dropped for being low. Five cells were flagged. The STFT-BLSTM 3-source cell was flagged at epoch 2 and cleared. The Conv-TasNet 3-source cell was flagged at epochs 2 and 4
($+3.63$ and $+4.11$~dB) and ended at $+4.62$~dB, so it was slow, not stuck. The Conv-TasNet 4-source cell stayed flat from epoch 4 to epoch 10 ($+3.44$ to $+3.47$~dB), and a screen of three rates
changed nothing. The DPRNN 3-source run at $10^{-3}$ stayed at $+3.05$~dB for four epochs; a screen of four rates favoured $5\times10^{-4}$ over $10^{-3}$ by $0.51$~dB at epoch 2
(borderline by the preset rule of $0.5$~dB, supported by the monotone trend with the learning rate and by the stuck run), and the cell was restarted at $5\times10^{-4}$, which cost four epochs
(about 2~h with pauses). The restarted run was flagged again at epoch 4 by the gap ($0.70$~dB) but was rising ($+0.47$~dB since epoch 2) and ended at $+4.92$~dB (validation). The DPRNN 4-source cell was flagged at epoch 4
($+3.38$~dB, rise $+0.07$~dB); a screen of three rates on 10,000 crops showed no winner (best $+0.20$~dB over $10^{-3}$), the run continued and ended at $+3.41$~dB.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

Every method is scored on one random 7,680-sample window of each test signal (shorter
signals in full), chosen by a fixed seed (crop seed 0) and the sample index, which is the
crop definition of the validation protocol. The same 7,526, 5,324 and 2,150 test samples are used for
the 2-, 3- and 4-source columns, against the
references described above. Intervals are 95\% bootstrap intervals over samples, and
differences between methods are paired bootstraps over the same samples. Three further passes
(the first 7,680 samples of each signal, and random windows with seeds 1 and 2) change the gain of
no 2-source model by more than 0.06~dB and of no 3- or 4-source model by more than 0.09~dB, and leave the order of the
models unchanged in every source count; ICA and NMF were scored in the first two passes only. The
first-window pass was run and read before the crop protocol was fixed; the headline is crop seed 0, and all four passes are
reported. All recipe decisions (learning rate, epoch budget, checkpoint) use the
validation split only. Two interim evaluations were run on the test split before the
final run: one on 600 two-source samples with an early checkpoint, and one of ICA and NMF on
the full test split. Neither was used to choose any setting.
Before the final runs we fixed one target for the training budget: a validation gain of at least $+6$~dB in the adjacent-channel, SNR${>}20$~dB bin after two epochs. No model's estimated gain reached it (best: STFT-BLSTM, $+5.68$~dB, 95\% interval $[+4.65,+6.73]$, which includes $+6$~dB). Exploratory runs of 10 epochs exceeded $+6$~dB, so the epoch budget was set from the validation curves, which departs from the pre-registered plan. We chose $E\!=\!10$ by a threshold fixed in advance: a 20-epoch probe of STFT-BLSTM on the validation split had to gain at least $0.30$~dB over the 10-epoch run in the all-samples bin. It gained $0.20$~dB (95\% interval $[0.15,0.25]$). Within the probe, the second 10 epochs added $0.26$~dB ($[0.20,0.31]$), so 10 epochs under-train the models relative to 20; the threshold is a cost--benefit rule, not a convergence claim.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ============================================================

\textbf{Reference construction.} The released arrays store each source after the channel and
hardware impairments but before resampling, frequency shift and power scaling, so a
user has to rebuild the exact mixture terms with \texttt{build\_aligned\_references}.
Storing the final terms as well would remove that step in a later release.

\textbf{Evaluation scope.} All methods are scored on one 7,680-sample window of
each signal, and the metric is phase sensitive. A separator that returns the correct
waveform with a constant phase offset is penalized. Model comparisons rest on
three seeds per model (two sources) or one seed (three and four sources) at the chosen learning rate, from one
learning-rate grid per model. Seed 0 of the two-source runs also served to choose the learning rates, so its score is mildly optimistic; seeds 1 and 2
are clean replicates and score the same within 0.3~dB. The pilot runs use a cosine schedule that anneals to
almost zero, so no model is shown to have converged, and the epoch budget was set
from the validation curves after the pre-registered criterion was missed. A 20-epoch
probe of STFT-BLSTM gained 0.20~dB in the all-samples bin over 10 epochs on the
validation split, so some performance was left unused. The
3-source and 4-source models get fewer optimizer steps than the 2-source models because
each trains for the same number of epochs on a smaller subset.

\textbf{Adjacent-channel mode.} The mode shifts the sources by multiples of 2~MHz for
every standard and applies no filtering or adjacent-channel leakage model. It is a
frequency displacement and not a guard-banded channel plan: 97\% of the two-source
adjacent-channel mixtures (99.5\% of the three-source and 99.9\% of the four-source
ones) have overlapping nominal channel bands. For 1.8\% of the adjacent-channel mixtures
the mixture rate is below 4~MHz (GSM at 2.166~MHz and narrow LTE channels at 1.92 and 3.84~MHz), so the 2~MHz
shift wraps around the sampling band; the references are built with the same shift, so the
labels are exact, but these mixtures are effectively co-channel or folded scenes. A
future release could use standard-specific offsets with an adjacent-channel leakage model.

\textbf{Single-antenna (SISO) only.} The current dataset models each
source as a scalar complex waveform with no spatial structure. Real
multi-antenna receivers can exploit spatial degrees of freedom for
separation; a MIMO extension (e.g., $2\times2$ or $4\times4$ arrays with
spatial correlation per 3GPP TDL) would allow spatial separation methods
and come closer to deployed base-station hardware.

\textbf{Downlink waveforms only.} The dataset covers downlink signals for
2G through 5G. Uplink waveforms differ in modulation order, power control
behavior, and resource allocation; their inclusion would broaden the
dataset's relevance to monitoring and coexistence analysis. Similarly,
NB-IoT, LTE-M, sidelink (D2D), and ISAC waveforms are absent.

\textbf{Future directions.} Planned extensions include: (1) incorporating
6G candidate waveforms (OTFS~\cite{hadani2017otfs}, filtered
OFDM~\cite{abdoli2015fofdm}) as they approach standardization; (2) using
geometry-based channel models such as the QuaDRiGa
simulator~\cite{jaeckel2014quadriga} in place of the synthetic TDL profiles; (3) scaling to higher source counts and wider
bandwidth mixtures; and (4) integrating automatic modulation classification
labels to support joint separation and classification tasks.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We presented RFSS, 100,000 multi-source RF samples that cover GSM, UMTS, LTE and
5G NR. Each source is generated from its 3GPP specification, passed through its own
TDL fading channel and a chain of five hardware impairments, and mixed co-channel or
adjacent-channel. Every sample stores the per-source waveforms from which the exact mixture terms are
rebuilt, so a model can be trained with permutation-invariant supervision.

On the two-source test split the best deep model, the STFT-BLSTM, improves PI-SI-SINR by $+6.14$~dB
over the input mixture, against $+8.04$~dB for the ideal-ratio-mask oracle
and $-13.60$ and $-1.24$~dB for ICA and NMF. The three deep models keep the same order in every matched seed pair, with mean
paired differences of $0.24$ to $0.53$~dB; with three and four sources the gains fall to $+5.14$ and $+4.52$~dB for the best model
(one seed per cell). Training was sensitive to the
learning rate and, for some models, to the seed; the learning-rate screening is in
Table~\ref{tab:lr}.

These results are a reproducible baseline for later methods. Planned extensions
are MIMO spatial diversity, uplink waveforms and emerging 6G candidate signals. The
dataset, code and checkpoints are released for non-commercial use.
